## Quick answer

Use registration software for an event that needs a clear attendee list, capacity or useful pre-event information. Use paid ticketing when the church also needs to collect money, state fees, issue confirmations, manage refunds and reconcile income. Do not choose a paid platform merely because it looks professional: a simple free sign-up may be better for a low-risk internal event, while a larger public event needs a demonstrable payment, refund and finance workflow. The meaningful test is the full attendee journey from invitation to check-in and final reconciliation, not the ticket-page design.

Be clear about whether the event is free, donation-based, paid, pay-on-arrival or has subsidised places. Each model changes the questions about attendee data, cancellation, finance ownership and communication. A booking confirmation is not a substitute for agreed event terms or a named organiser.

## Choose registration or ticketing from the event model

Start with the event's actual decision: headcount only, attendance confirmation, paid entry, multiple ticket types, a waiting list, dietary/accessibility needs, children or family groups, workshops, or an on-door payment. State the capacity owner, cancellation route and the person responsible for the money before opening registrations.

| Event shape | Usually needs | Test before launch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Free internal gathering | Registration or existing church system | Capacity, reminders and no-show handling |
| Free public event | Public sign-up page | Privacy notice, duplicate sign-ups and waitlist |
| Paid one-off event | Ticketing and payment workflow | Fees, refunds, receipts and finance handover |
| Multi-session course | Registration plus attendance | Sequence, missed sessions and communications |
| Family/children's event | Appropriate guardian and child journey | Minimum data, group allocation and check-in boundary |

Do not make a child-registration form or attendee question field into a catch-all notes system. Collect only what is necessary for the event and use the church's appropriate route for sensitive information or a safeguarding concern.

## Compare the fee, refund and finance path

Ask suppliers to show the net amount the church receives, not merely the advertised ticket price. Identify payment-processing fees, service fees, VAT treatment where relevant, chargebacks, refunds and whether the buyer or church bears a fee. Compare like with like: an event platform's fee structure, payment provider and manual finance process can all affect the real cost.

ChurchSuite's current ticket documentation describes local-currency ticket prices, Stripe card payments, payment status and pay-on-arrival handling.[^churchsuite-tickets] Its check-in article describes seeing ticket, payment and event-question details in a check-in workflow.[^churchsuite-checkin] These are supplier-published capabilities to test, not a recommendation or a substitute for the church's finance controls.

If using Eventbrite, its public help explains how organisers can issue full or partial refunds and separately configure refund policies.[^eventbrite-refund] Policies and fees vary by provider and may change. Write the church's event-specific refund decision plainly and make sure the system can put it into practice; do not copy a platform default because it is available.

## Test the whole attendee journey

Run two fictional tests: a free event and a paid event. Include a cancellation and an attendee who needs help. The test should demonstrate what users, event volunteers and the finance contact each see.

1. Publish a simple event page with the approved information, privacy route and contact option.
2. Register a test attendee and confirm the email or message is clear and accessible.
3. Complete a paid test using the planned provider and note gross amount, fees, net amount and reference.
4. Change capacity, add a waiting-list scenario or close sales, then check the public result.
5. Cancel or refund a test order and confirm what the attendee, organiser and finance record show.
6. Check in attendees with a restricted volunteer account and test an unregistered arrival.
7. Export a final attendance and payment report, then reconcile it to the agreed finance record.

The trial should expose on-door realities: poor connection, a dead phone, a person who cannot find their confirmation, a card-payment limitation or an event volunteer who needs help. Agree a paper or offline fallback where appropriate and do not grant broad financial access merely to speed up a queue.

## Keep attendee information proportionate and controlled

The ICO's data-minimisation guidance requires personal data to be adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary.[^ico] For an ordinary ticket, a name and contact route may be enough. Ask why any optional field is needed, who will see it, how long it is retained and whether it is shared with a payment or ticketing provider.

Set separate permissions for event setup, attendee check-in, refund approval, finance reporting and account administration. Event volunteers often need a name and attendance status, not the full set of answers or payment information. If a question exposes health, pastoral or safeguarding information, do not assume it belongs in a general attendee list.

## Implement, review and close the event

Publish a concise operating note covering ticket types, fee display, refund authority, event contact, on-door fallback, check-in owner and reconciliation deadline. Train the team against the tested journey. After the event, reconcile attendance, income, refunds and outstanding action; then apply the church's records approach to attendee data, unused lists and exports.

Review the public experience as well as internal administration. Was the event easy to find, could a person register without a particular app, were fees clear, did reminders reach the right people and could staff resolve a problem respectfully? Keep the evidence needed for finance and event management, but do not retain a marketing list simply because it was exported from a ticket platform.

If the event is repeated, copy the tested operating model rather than copying an old event blindly. Recheck date, capacity, ticket price, refund wording, permissions, access needs and finance contact. A template is helpful only when its assumptions still match the next event. When a provider changes a fee, payment method or policy, repeat the fee-and-refund part of the trial before opening sales.

Avoid promising a response time that the church cannot staff. State one contact route and make responsibility visible between the event lead, office and finance team. A clear, timely human answer to an attendee is usually more valuable than adding another automated email.
