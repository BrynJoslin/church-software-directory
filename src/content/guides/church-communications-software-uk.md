## Quick answer

Choose communications software by deciding which channel owns each job. Email is usually suitable for fuller, searchable information and formal notices; messaging can work for timely coordination in a bounded group; an app or member portal can provide an owned place for ongoing resources and updates. None should become an unofficial substitute for the others. The problem is rarely a lack of channels. It is scattered contact lists, unclear audience rules, inaccessible messages and volunteers using personal accounts because the approved route is too difficult.

Start with a communication operating model: audience, purpose, channel, sender, approval, preference and archive. Then choose the smallest toolset that supports it. A product that sends email, SMS and push notifications is not automatically a better fit if the church cannot explain who may use each one and how people change their preferences.

## Assign a clear job to every channel

List real messages from the past month. Group them by purpose rather than by the person who happened to send them: weekly news, rota reminders, urgent cancellation, pastoral follow-up, event registration, group chat, safeguarding information, public invitation and member-only update. For each group, decide whether it is public, internal, optional, operational or urgent.

| Communication need | Usually needs | Decision to document |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Weekly church news | Email or portal/app | Who edits, which list and how preferences apply? |
| Rota change | Targeted email/SMS/app alert | Who can see the team and how is urgency handled? |
| Urgent building closure | Direct, time-bound route | Who authorises it and what is the fallback? |
| Group conversation | Bounded messaging group | Who moderates, joins and removes members? |
| Public event | Website and registration route | What information is public and who handles replies? |

This prevents a group-chat channel becoming the de facto membership system or an app notification being used for every low-priority message. It also protects people who do not have a smartphone, do not use a particular platform or need information in another format.

## Choose a source of truth for people and preferences

Name one system as the source for contact details, group membership and communication preferences. If a team keeps its own spreadsheet or personal WhatsApp contacts alongside the church database, decide how it is reviewed, minimised and retired. A new app will not repair fragmented data if each team imports a separate list.

ChurchSuite's public communication documentation describes email and SMS from its modules, privacy settings and ongoing consent options.[^churchsuite] Its current preference documentation says options are respected by default and distinguishes rota reminders from general communications.[^churchsuite-preferences] That describes supplier functionality; it does not tell a church when it may override a preference or what message is appropriate.

The ICO's current electronic-mail guidance addresses direct marketing under PECR, including a 2026 update relevant to charities.[^ico-pecr] Church notices, operational messages and fundraising can be legally and practically different. Obtain appropriate data-protection advice for the church's own purposes instead of assuming that a single opt-in label settles every message.

## Compare access, accessibility and operational control

Ask each supplier to demonstrate the church's own audience model. Test a communications administrator, ministry leader, rota volunteer and ordinary member account. Check which lists each can use, what personal details are visible, whether messages can be approved or scheduled, and whether a mistaken send can be stopped or corrected.

Treat accessibility as a requirement. A beautiful app is not a replacement for a readable email, simple web page, printed notice or personal contact where needed. Test links, text size, colour contrast, keyboard use and the ability to understand a message without an image or push alert. Keep urgent messages concise and give a route for replies or help.

Ask how the tool handles staff departures, a lost phone, changing group membership, exports, failed delivery and an account closure. The right answer may be a documented manual process rather than an automated integration.

## Trial the communication architecture, not just a send button

Use fictional or approved test recipients. Run these tasks before committing:

1. Send a weekly update to a test audience with different stated preferences.
2. Send a targeted rota reminder and confirm that it is distinct from general promotion.
3. Publish an event notice to a public page without exposing member information.
4. Add and remove a person from a ministry group, then check their access and notifications.
5. Deliver a time-bound urgent notice and use the agreed non-digital fallback.
6. View messages on a small phone, keyboard-only browser and an ordinary email client.
7. Export or close the test account, confirming who owns contacts and message history.

Review message volume after the trial. If people ignore alerts, the answer may be fewer, clearer channel rules—not another notification setting. Train leaders to use the approved system and make exceptions explicit rather than silently moving sensitive or urgent communication into personal accounts.

## Implement with an owned communication policy

Publish a lightweight policy: which channels are approved; what each is for; who owns groups and lists; how preferences and access are updated; what must not be posted; how people request correction or removal; and where urgent or safeguarding concerns go instead. Review the policy whenever a new app, social platform or SMS provider is introduced.

The first review should measure practical outcomes: did the right people receive the message, could they understand it, did group membership remain current, and did volunteers avoid side channels? Remove duplicate lists and unused channels only after the new route has proved reliable and a controlled archive or export has been agreed.

Keep a simple communications calendar for predictable activity, but do not turn it into an automatic-send machine. Every planned message should still have an owner, a stated audience and a reason for that channel. If a message contains a private pastoral matter, a safeguarding concern or a complaint, route it to the church's appropriate procedure rather than sending it through a group list or app notification.

Record the decision in a place that future leaders can find. It should survive a change of staff, a new messaging platform or a volunteer handover without resetting communication practice to personal contacts and informal assumptions.
