## Quick answer

Choose sermon hosting or podcast software by deciding what the church is publishing: a searchable sermon archive, an audio podcast, video teaching, selected service recordings or a live stream. These are related but different operating models. A podcast host distributes episodes through an RSS feed; a video platform has a different public-discovery and privacy model; a church archive may need speakers, series, Bible references and an embed on the church website. Avoid assuming that one upload is a complete publishing strategy. Keep original media files, publication permission, titles and metadata under church control so that a future move is possible.

Before publishing, agree who approves a recording, who owns the account, how the church deals with an error or removal request, what access needs are met and when recordings are reviewed or removed. Software cannot supply permission from a preacher or solve copyright in music or other material captured in a service.

## Choose the publication model and boundary

Write a short definition for each output. A sermon archive may contain edited teaching only. A podcast may distribute audio episodes through RSS to several listening apps. A video channel may host a sermon or a whole service. A livestream has live consent, moderation and reliability requirements. Do not merge these simply because a supplier offers all of them.

The Methodist Church's recording guidance notes that copyright in a sermon or address belongs to the person who wrote and delivered it, and says permission should be sought before recording and publishing.[^methodist] That is a strong practical starting point. Ask speakers during invitation or booking, not moments before a camera is switched on. Make it easy to decline or limit publication.

Set an archive boundary: whole services, sermon-only edits, selected series, or audio-only. Decide whether each is public, unlisted, member-only, time-limited or permanently retained. The narrower and clearer the model, the easier it is to manage permissions, captions, corrections and exit.

## Compare ownership, discovery and exit

The central question is not just where a recording can be uploaded. It is whether the church can identify its content, move it, update it and remove it. Ask each supplier for a current explanation of account ownership, exports, original-file storage, RSS feed, redirects, embed options, metadata, deletion route and support when an administrator leaves.

Spotify for Creators says it creates an RSS feed when the first episode is published for a podcast hosted there.[^spotify-rss] Its terms place responsibility on users to hold the rights and permissions for uploaded content.[^spotify-terms] Those public statements are useful evidence, but the church should still check the current migration and ownership route for its chosen plan.

SermonAudio publicly describes a platform for audio and video preaching, livestreaming and cross-publishing.[^sermonaudio] Test that particular workflow if it is relevant; do not infer a feature, price, archive guarantee or rights clearance from a general platform description.

| Decision area | Evidence to request | Question to settle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Account ownership | Named account owner and recovery process | Can the church retain control if a volunteer leaves? |
| Original files | Download and backup route | Where is the master copy held? |
| Podcast distribution | RSS feed and directory process | Can listeners keep finding the show after a move? |
| Website archive | Embed and searchable metadata | Can the church present the archive on its own domain? |
| Removal | Take-down and correction test | Who can act quickly on an error or request? |
| Exit | Sample export and contract terms | What remains portable and readable? |

## Design for accessible, accurate publishing

Audio quality is usually more important than camera polish. Test speech on a phone, headphones and a weak connection. For video, enable captions where available and correct material errors in important recordings. Give a simple text description, title, speaker, date, Bible reference and series information so that people can decide whether the content is relevant.

The Methodist accessibility guide explains that captions benefit more than one audience and that an audio-only experience should still make sense.[^methodist] Avoid visual references that make an audio recording incomprehensible. If a slide, reading or diagram is essential, provide an accessible accompanying resource where appropriate.

Record only what the church can maintain. A small, consistent audio archive with useful titles may be more valuable than a video channel containing every service with unclear permissions and no maintenance owner.

## Trial a complete publication and removal workflow

Use a test recording or material whose permissions are clear. Run the entire workflow before publishing a back catalogue.

1. Capture an audio and video test and store the original file in the church's agreed location.
2. Add accurate speaker, title, date, series and Bible-reference metadata.
3. Publish to the proposed host and, where relevant, generate or check the RSS feed.
4. Embed or link it from a stable page on the church website.
5. Check captions, audio clarity, mobile playback and a listener's ability to find the episode.
6. Correct a title and remove the test recording, confirming how long the change takes to appear.
7. Download or export the test material and document the steps for a future host move.

The trial should include the preacher or service owner, the technical volunteer and the person who maintains the website. It is not complete until all three understand their part.

## Implement an archive and review routine

Create a short publishing checklist: permission confirmed, content checked, title and metadata complete, music or third-party material considered, accessibility route prepared, destination selected, and archive review date set. Give the account and source-file store at least two named church-controlled administrators. Do not leave a password, payment method or RSS feed under a former volunteer's personal account.

Set a review rhythm. Remove accidental recordings, correct factual errors, assess old series for ongoing usefulness and check that links still work. The ICO explains that people can have a right to erasure in particular circumstances; establish a route for recognising and responding to requests rather than promising automatic deletion.[^ico]

Keep an editorial record alongside the media: who approved publication, any speaker restriction, relevant licence note, original filename and the date of the next review. This modest discipline makes a future correction, archive migration or removal request much easier to handle without guessing what happened years earlier.
