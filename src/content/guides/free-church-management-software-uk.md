Free church management software is useful when it gives a church time to test a real workflow. It is risky when “free” is treated as proof that the service will meet UK data, giving, support or exit needs.

## Quick answer

Free can be a sensible starting point, but it describes a price model rather than a fit or a risk level. UK churches should distinguish a free tier of a hosted service, a permanently free supplier product and open-source software where the church runs the service itself. Compare the capacity limit, paid path, technical responsibility, data evidence, support, export and continuity before uploading live records. This guide was researched on 25 July 2026 from supplier and authoritative material; it is not a ranking or hands-on test.

## Scope and method

This guide covers free or free-entry tools for people, rota and basic church administration. It does not establish that any product supports UK Gift Aid, safeguarding administration, accounting or data protection compliance. The examples are representative directory entries with current first-party free-model statements. The comparison criteria are the free model, workflow boundary, costs that may arrive later, ownership and exit—not a feature count.

## The cost that does not disappear

Free plans often charge through capacity, transaction fees, paid modules, hardware, messages, migration or the team’s time. Open-source software moves more of that cost into technical responsibility. Neither approach is wrong; both need to be budgeted.

Do not upload live pastoral, safeguarding or finance data to a free trial before checking account controls, processor terms, data location or transfer information, export and cancellation. Religious belief is special category data under UK GDPR. <a href="https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/special-category-data/what-is-special-category-data/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">ICO guidance</a>.

## Choose the free model deliberately

| Model | May suit | Material trade-off | First question to settle |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hosted free tier: Planning Center, ChMeetings or ChurchLinker | A church testing defined workflows with limited contacts or usage. | Capacity, paid modules, currency and service terms can change the long-term cost. | Threshold, renewal route, permissions and usable export. |
| Supplier says all features are free: B1 Church or WorshipTools | A church with a narrow, evidenced workflow to test. | Support, continuity, UK-fit and excluded finance/giving work remain material. | Current terms, support route and data handling documentation. |
| Open source: ChurchCRM | A church with reliable technical stewardship or a paid technical host. | The church owns hosting, patching, backup, recovery and incident response. | Named owner, documented maintenance and restoration test. |

Free is not always the lowest-risk choice. A short paid trial may cost less than a difficult migration away from an unowned free system. Conversely, a free tier can be a responsible option when it is deliberately limited, regularly reviewed and backed by a simple exit plan.

## A sensible free-software test

Use fictional records and a short test: add people, give a leader restricted access, make a rota, export the database and identify the paid threshold. Then ask: who will own it after the enthusiastic tester moves on?

Free is a good reason to trial. It is not a reason to skip procurement.

## Ownership, implementation and exit

Name one administrator and a backup before creating the account. Record what information belongs in the service, which roles can see or export it, how new volunteers are trained and what happens when someone leaves. Use fictional or minimised records in a trial. A product cannot give the church a retention policy, a safeguarding boundary or a data-access process.

Test a complete exit while the account is still empty: export the fictional records, open them without the product, identify retention/archive needs and read cancellation or deletion terms. For an open-source route, test restoring a backup rather than assuming one exists. The NCSC advises buyers to seek confidence proportionate to the sensitivity of their data and to look for evidence rather than accepting supplier assertions alone.

## UK checks before live use

Confirm the billing and support route even where the plan is free: a free account can still lead to US-dollar paid add-ons, card-processing fees or a service with no UK-specific tax or Gift Aid workflow. If donations are involved, trace a fictional declaration, payment, fee and finance report. If personal data is involved, ask for current processing terms, permissions, sub-processors and exit provisions. A church should not call a free product suitable for UK compliance merely because it can be accessed from the UK.

The proportionate answer may be a clearly bounded free tool alongside an existing process, with a scheduled review when contact numbers, risk or workload change.

## Your next step

Define one problem, choose one free-tier candidate and one alternative, and run the same fictional-data test. Take the resulting evidence, paid thresholds, ownership and exit plan to the people responsible for finance and data. If the church cannot sustain the service after the initial enthusiast moves on, do not treat free as a saving.

Schedule a review before the free limit is reached, not after. Check whether the tool still fits the real workload, whether access remains appropriate and whether a paid route, simpler process or specialist tool would now reduce risk. Continuity is a decision to make deliberately.

Keep an independent record of the account owner, recovery address and billing contact. The church should be able to regain control when a volunteer changes role, even if no subscription invoice has ever been paid.

Consider whether the free plan’s public support and documentation are sufficient for the people who will operate it. If the answer depends on one technically confident volunteer, record that dependency plainly and make a backup plan.
